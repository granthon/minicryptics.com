If you've never tackled a cryptic crossword, they can look bewildering.  The clues read like gibberish, and they don't seem to have anything at all to do with the answers!  Fear not: there is a system, and - like a conjuror's trick - once you've seen how it works, it's hard to un-see it. Small grids are a great place to learn. There are just a handful of short answers, so you can get through a whole 5×5 mini in five minutes or so, once you've got the hang of it, and nothing is too much of a slog when you haven't.

Every example on this page comes from a puzzle on this site. The explanations are hidden, so hover over or tap the blacked-out bits when you want to see them.

## Two clues in one

Almost every cryptic clue has two parts:

- a **definition**, which is an ordinary, if sometimes sneaky, crossword clue for the answer. It is usually at the very start or the very end of the clue.
- some **wordplay**, which builds the same answer another way: by sticking smaller words together, scrambling letters, hiding the answer in plain sight, and so on.

The number in brackets is the **enumeration**: the number of letters in the answer, with (3, 4) meaning two words of three and four letters.

The trick is that the clue reads as a sensible phrase — the *surface reading* — which has nothing to do with the eventual answer or how it actually works. Take this one, from [5×5 #25](/puzzle/xjihlpmowz1whmwzw4ax/):

> Freight vehicle at exit (5)

On the surface, it's about a lorry leaving a depot. But split it up: "freight" is the definition, and the wordplay is "vehicle" = CAR, followed by "exit" = GO. Put them together and you get CARGO, which is indeed freight. That's the whole game: find the join between definition and wordplay, then you can ignore the surface meaning entirely.

## The main clue types

Clues use a fairly small set of tricks. Words in the clue that tell you which trick is in play are called **indicators**, and learning to spot them is most of the battle.  Here are some of the most common ones that you should expect to come across:

### Charades

Little pieces, put together in order, like CAR-GO up above.

> Feathers cause fruit to ripen (7) — from [7×7 #7](/puzzle/dk2bbtnahqlrx9oanhs0/)

||"fruit" = PLUM + "to ripen" = AGE makes PLUMAGE, defined by "feathers."||

There is no indicator here: words in the clue are simply replaced with short synonyms.

### Anagrams

The letters of some words are rearranged. There are hundreds of possible indicators, but look for anything suggesting disorder, error, damage, cooking, dancing, drunkenness or the like: "off", "wild", "cooked", "badly", "swinging"…

> *Titanic* set him off: "so will nine be saved?" (1, 6, 2, 4) — from [13×13 #3](/puzzle/bmpzfi9sbtnqk0gp8orf/)

||An anagram ("off") of TITANIC SET HIM — A STITCH IN TIME, which as the proverb goes, saves nine.||

### Hidden words

The answer is sitting right there in the clue, running across the gaps between words. Indicators include "some", "in", "part of", "holding" or "eating".

> Euclideans were grasping solution (6) 

||Hidden ("grasping") in "euclideANSWERe" — ANSWER, defined by "solution."||

### Double definitions

Two definitions of the same word, side by side, with no wordplay at all.

> Allow entry to make a confession (5) — from [5×5 #22](/puzzle/4udayk0ubf1a0pp8thea/)

||"Allow entry" = ADMIT, and "make a confession" = ADMIT.||

Sometimes these clues can be as short as two words:

> Low average (4) - from [9×9 #7](/puzzle/nlljyspzk8heofam2fne/)

||"Low" = MEAN, as in, base; nasty; despicable.  "Average" = MEAN, as in the mathematical average obtained by summing n numbers and dividing by n.||

Rarely, you might see variants of this idea with triple definitions, quadruples, or even higher numbers.

### Containers

One piece goes inside another. Watch for "in", "taking in", "holding", "about", "outside", "grabs", "swallowing" and similar words.

> 'Moderate' King taking in Hollywood location (5) — [5×5 #51](/puzzle/c05lmzgubjeccs73jurg/)

||"Hollywood location" = LA, inside "King" = REX — RELAX, "moderate".||

### Reversals

A word written backwards: "back", "returning", "recalled", "on the way back", or, in a Down clue, "up" or "rising".

> Tuber recalled days of spring (3) — [5×5 #46](/puzzle/bi8bo3czuw6psriynim9/)

||"Tuber" = YAM, reversed ("recalled") — MAY.||

### Homophone (Sounds like)

The answer sounds like something else, albeit often in a far-fetched way. Look out for indicators to do with hearing, such as "we hear", "on the radio", "overheard", "reportedly".

> Overheard elevated murmuring sound of poetry (5) — [5×5 #49](/puzzle/akms91vct3e0dn44oh0t/)

||Sounds like ("overheard") "elevated" = HIGH + "murmuring sound" = COO — HAIKU, a form of poetry.||

### Deletions

Take a letter, or a bit, away from a word: "endless", "doesn't end", "headless", "without...", "losing...", etc.  Often, the centre of a word is removed to leave its first and last letters: "empty bottle" = B~~ottl~~E; "schoolboy on vacation" = S~~choolbo~~Y

> Contest doesn't end level (4) — [11×11 #5](/puzzle/elvqwwg1zk0jqaqygkjx/)

||"Contest" = EVENT, without its end — EVEN, "level".||

### Letter selection

Use particular letters from the clue: first letters ("initially", "heads", "leaders"), last letters ("finally", "tails"), outside letters ("case", "edges", "empty"), or every other letter ("odd", "even", "regularly").

> Some of Rodney's toys initially put in order (4) — [5×5 #24](/puzzle/xtsmybramgd95pb7bipn/)

||The initial letters of "Some Of Rodney's Toys" — SORT, "put in order".||

### Cryptic definitions

No wordplay: just a single definition with a misleading twist. A question mark at the end is often a hint that something like this is going on, but don't count on it.

> London's premier residential location (7, 6) — [13×13 #3](/puzzle/bmpzfi9sbtnqk0gp8orf/)

||The London residence of the premier, i.e. the Prime Minister — DOWNING STREET.||

### All-in-one clues

The rarest, and (some say) most satisfying: the whole clue is the definition *and* the whole clue does double-duty as word play as well. Setters and solvers call these *&lit*, which stands for "and literally true."  Any of the above wordplay types could be employed in an &lit clue.

> They're foremost in altitude, lofty peaks, and snow (4) — [5×5 #45](/puzzle/b8oqitnouugme2pj76y4/)

||The first letters ("foremost") of "Altitude, Lofty Peaks" and "Snow" spell ALPS; and the whole clue describes them too.||

### Rarities and others

This list is by no means exhaustive - there are plenty of other tricks hiding up the setter's sleeve.  Spoonerisms, cockneyisms (often dropped H's), palindromes, letter-cycling, rebuses and more.  But of course, part of the joy of crosswords is in learning as you go.

## Common abbreviations

Charades lean heavily on short bits of crosswordese. A few that turn up a lot in these puzzles:

- **One**: I (or A)
- **Small**: S
- **Right**, **left**: R, L
- **Conservative**, **Liberal**: C, L
- **Doctor**: DR (or MO, MB)
- **Church**: CH
- **Gold**: OR (from heraldry) AU (from the periodic table)
- **Artist**: RA (Royal Academician)
- **Commercial**: AD
- **Former partner**: EX
- **That is**: IE
- **Energy**, **drug**: E
- **New, Knight**: N
- **Time**: T
- **King**: K, R, or REX
- **Setter**, **me**: ME, I; **setter's** is MY

You'll pick up the rest as you go, and on this site, the setter's notes on each puzzle spell them out.

## Getting started

- **Seek the definition.** It's usually at one end of the clue, so try the first word or two, then the last.
- **Look for indicators.** "Cooked", "inside", "back" and "we hear" are big signposts.
- **Count the letters.** If the bit next to an anagram indicator has exactly as many letters as the answer, you're probably onto something.
- **Use the crossers.** Every letter you get makes the clues it crosses easier; a 5×5 grid fills up fast.
- **Don't be too proud to check.** The puzzles let you check or reveal letters, and every puzzle here has setter's notes explaining every clue.
